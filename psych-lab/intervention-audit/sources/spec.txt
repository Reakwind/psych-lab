[001] PD psychology program
[002] Introduction
[003] This psychological program is designed as an integrated component of a broader digital therapeutic experience for people with Parkinson’s disease (PwP). Its purpose is not only to address psychological symptoms, but also to support the day-to-day behaviors that make multidisciplinary treatment effective over time. The psychological program is intended to help users 1) cope more effectively with the emotional and psychological burden of PD, through a supportive framework and a toolkit of relevant practices that users can learn practice and integrate into daily life; 2) engage with movement, physical therapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy, cognitive training, and medication-management; and 3) build a sustainable daily routine that can continue after the initial structured 12-week phase. Notably, the app is not trying to deliver a traditional therapist-led CBT or mindfulness course. It is trying to support daily participation in a complex rehabilitation environment, with no live therapist and minimal writing or conversational interaction.
[004] Clinical rationale
[005] Parkinson's affects a wide range of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral processes. People living with Parkinson’s often face challenges such as anxiety, depression, emotional dysregulation, attention difficulties, apathy, reduced motivation, identity shifts, and adjustment difficulties related to the disease’s progression and its impact on daily life. In more advanced stages, there can also be psychosis (as an undesired result of the dopaminergic treatment), impulse control issues, and cognitive decline.
[006] These "non-motor symptoms" are in part shaped by the experience of living with PD, but many of them are direct symptoms of the condition itself – often appearing before the motor symptoms. They can significantly affect quality of life, independence, daily functioning, relationships, and adherence to the therapeutic program. They can even amplify motor symptoms (for example - anxiety or sleep difficulties may exacerbate tremors and freezing), creating a reinforcing cycle of distress and functional decline.
[007] A psychological component is especially important in PD because emotional and motivational difficulties are not peripheral to the condition. Anxiety, low mood, stress, apathy, shame, guilt reduced confidence, and social withdrawal can all interfere with rehabilitation and daily functioning. The program acknowledges and validates these challenges, bringing them into awareness while offering practical ways to respond, alongside a sense of support and encouragement. The central challenge this psychological program is designed to address is not just “mental health symptoms,” but the moment-to-moment gap between intention and action. A user may value movement, believe it is important, and still fail to begin. They may want to use the speech program but avoid it because of embarrassment or low confidence. They may know that sleep affects tomorrow’s functioning but fail to implement a settling routine because the day already feels like a loss. The program is designed around these gaps.
[008] The program combines several therapeutic traditions in a practical way:
[009] CBT-informed elements are used for unhelpful thoughts, worry, self-criticism, sleep, and practical coping.
[010] Behavioral activation is used to address apathy, reduced activity, low momentum, and the tendency to wait for motivation before acting.
[011] Mindfulness and physiological regulation are used to help users settle stress and re-engage attention.
[012] ACT (acceptance and commitment therapy)-informed elements are introduced in simplified form to support purpose, willingness, values-based action, and psychological flexibility.
[013] Self-compassion and pacing are used to reduce boom-bust cycles, shame, and dropout after difficult days.
[014] Graded confidence-building is used to support participation in movement, speech, cognition, and social action.
[015] This combination is especially appropriate in PD because the app needs to support both emotional wellbeing and adherence behavior. In addition, to some extent, there is an aspect of a journey to the psychological content, with later content building upon earlier concepts, allowing users to gradually expand their skills, understanding, and application over time.
[016] In addition, the program incorporates two supporting approaches that are useful in a non-conversational, therapist-free digital environment.
[017] The first is an MI-informed motivational layer. The app does not attempt to replicate formal motivational interviewing, since it cannot provide live reflective listening or responsive dialogue. Instead, it uses structured MI-consistent elements: eliciting personally meaningful reasons, acknowledging ambivalence without confrontation, supporting autonomy, strengthening confidence, and helping users choose the next step rather than merely deciding whether or not to act. This is designed to increase adherence and persistence, which are shaped by self-efficacy, confidence, meaning, fatigue, and support as much as by knowledge of what is “good to do.”
[018] The second is the careful use of therapeutic suggestion. This is not formal hypnosis, and the program should not be framed that way. Rather, it uses brief supportive suggestions that shape attention, bodily settling, expectancy, and readiness for action. In practice, this means language that helps users orient to the present moment, soften bodily tension, anticipate manageable success, and enter tasks with less friction. Suggestive wording is particularly well suited to audio-guided practices, calming sequences, and confidence-building prompts. In a condition where initiation, bodily tension, uncertainty, and symptom-related apprehension can all interfere with action, such phrasing can make the psychological layer feel more immediate and embodied. Suggestive language should be used to support attention, calm, confidence, and readiness, not to make unrealistic promises or imply magical control over symptoms. Suitable uses include:
[019] settling the body before tasks
[020] post-practice prompts
[021] increasing expectancy of manageable success
[022] focusing attention on one step at a time
[023] softening self-critical interpretations
[024] reinforcing bodily cues of readiness
[025] pleasant familiarity over different practices (voice, movement, etc.) starting with a pleasant welcome, taking a calming breath, ending with a thank you, an encouragement, or a "for the rest of the day…".
[026] Importantly, the program also aims to strengthen users' perception that every interaction with the app is itself a meaningful act of managing their Parkinson's. The messaging and overall tone should help users recognize that every practice they complete, every activity they engage in, and every time they return to the app is already an active step toward better self-management, healthier habits, and improved daily functioning. Over time, the app should reinforce the feeling that "I am someone who is actively doing something for my health," helping users see themselves as more active, engaged, and capable.
[027] Because Parkinson’s disease is highly variable, the psychological program is intentionally broad. It is designed to address a wide range of emotional, cognitive, and functional challenges that people with Parkinson’s may face in daily life. The program aims to better equip users with knowledge, tools, habits, and practical strategies to manage difficulties (unhelpful emotional and cognitive patterns, harmful habits or self-talk and more) or positively put- living healthier life with PD and improving quality of life.
[028] While some patients may also develop deeper insight as a result of the psychology program, in essence it is mainly trying to produce:
[029] lower self-blame
[030] higher self-efficacy
[031] higher willingness to start
[032] better emotional regulation before tasks
[033] more flexible persistence on bad days
[034] stronger identity and meaning around continued practice
[035] long-term habit formation with a low burden interface
[036] Increased involvement with life
[037] Note that the same principles and language can and should be consistently applied not only within the psychological program but also within all the other programs in the app (physical therapy, occupational therapy, dance therapy, speech therapy, cognitive training, etc.).
[038] Design Principles
[039] The psychological program has a “standalone” component to it, but it is also an adherence engine - The psychology layer should support use of the rest of the app. It should not feel like “extra homework” competing with the rehabilitation content.
[040] Daily interaction should be low-burden and mostly non-writing based - The program is designed for video, audio, games, and tap-based input. Free-text writing is minimized. This is especially appropriate for PD because handwriting, typing, fatigue, and cognitive load can all make long journaling tasks impractical, especially in a self-guided format.
[041] In line with the approach taken across the entire therapeutic program, the psychological program emphasizes learning with application. Ideally, each concept, technique, or therapeutic principle introduced is accompanied by opportunities for practice and integration. This may take the form of interactive exercises, structured reflections, or real-life tasks that are brought back into the app through self-reporting and feedback. The goal (and challenge) is to make therapeutic content not only informative, but usable, engaging, and relevant to users’ daily experiences.
[042] Safety and escalation need to be built in - Because the app is self-guided, it should include basic information on when to seek additional support, including in mental health aspects (severe anxiety, depression, hallucinations, etc.,). This can also be referred throughout the program in places where it makes sense to (e.g., in the unit talking about anxiety).
[043] Theme (within the first 12 weeks) - Each unit focuses on one main psychological or behavioral skill, taught through one anchor “lesson” and repeated daily micro-practices.
[044] Logic for missed days – the anchor should be prioritized and not to be missed.
[045] Program High-Level Structure
[046] The psychological program is organized into units, each addressing specific themes, topics, or therapeutic targets relevant to individuals living with Parkinson’s. Notably, the psychological program follows a more linear and progressive structure compared to other domains, at least in the initial 12 weeks, which are more structured. As we move to the maintenance phase, users can continue to practice the psychological tools and routines they most connected with.
[047] In the first 12 weeks, each unit has one main theme, but the theme is taught through repetition and practical use. The unit rhythm is:
[048] one “anchor lesson” at the start of the unit- ± 3 minutes long
[049] one related short daily interaction
[050] Some of these will be additional “learning” to reinforce the message and place it in additional contexts
[051] Some of these will be practices that can be used repeatedly over time - also beyond the unit in which they were introduced --> note that some of the practices are longer (up to 7 minutes), will be marked below as longer
[052] optional additional content patients can access if interested
[053] In addition, the themes can be used within other modules in the app (e.g., the physiotherapist can speak about action before motivation, the occupational therapist can refer to pacing, etc.) + they can inform reusable boosters linked to that unit’s theme – see above the note about creating visuals that can be repeated throughout the app.
[054] The intention is that users meet the same ideas in slightly different forms: watch it once, practice it repeatedly, use it while doing other therapy, and then eventually internalize it as part of their routine. While it is expected that the “learning” sections will not necessarily be something the patients go back to, the practices should be designed to be used repeatedly.
[055] Types of activities
[056] The psychology program includes the following types of assets:
[057] Educational – these are designed to provide information. They may be delivered in various formats, such as video, animated video, slides, articles, etc. They are passive in nature and are not designed to be used many times.
[058] Practices – these are designed as tools that the patients that they learn and repeat over time, as self-care tools. These can be, for example, breathing exercises, relaxation/mindfulness audios, collecting a positive moment, etc.
[059] Interactive- these are designed to help patients internalize the education they have been exposed to. The goal is to turn them from passive viewers to more active “learners”. These may include, for example – asking a question and letting the patient choose from a list of answers, adopting certain ideas/thoughts and rejecting others, marking a key idea to “keep” etc. These mechanisms may be shared across modules. They are not designed as self-help tools or activities that the patient is likely to go back to and can follow the educational or the practice content.
[060] Visual anchors – this is a part of the educational content, however it is spelled out separately because of its importance and applicability beyond the piece of educational content it appears in originally. The idea is to create several key visual anchors. For example, show a visual of a bull’s eye to signify how close an action is to core values, and then use the visual later when asking about value-guided actions in the daily protocol. Or use a visual of an imbalance between perceived effort and perceived reward when explaining apathy, and then repeat this visual when the physical therapist talks about the daily physio practice.
[061] A focus on practices
[062] The psychological content is structured into modules (weeks), each covering themes that support the patient journey. Alongside the modules, users are introduced to practices that they learn and repeat over time, either in their original form or with variations adapted to each module’s theme. These practices also function as standalone self-care tools.
[063] Conceptual note: The practices are connected and support each other. Each emphasizes a different aspect of mind–body regulation while supporting the others.
[064] Relaxation practices emphasize ease and physical comfort, which supports breathing and mindfulness.
[065] Breathing practices are embedded within both relaxation and mindfulness that often call for intentional breathing.
[066] Mindfulness introduces acceptance and non-judgmental awareness, which enhances the effectiveness of both relaxation and breathing practices.
[067] Core/optional practices
[068] Core practices are integrated into daily activities and repeated throughout the program, sometimes with variations aligned to module themes.
[069] Additional practices are offered for users who want to deepen their engagement, understanding, and practice.(e.g., after practicing two short breathing exercises, the user can access two additional optional breathing techniques; or after practicing guided imagery for relaxation, the user can try guided imagery for a safe place or a longer version for sleep).

[102] Cognitive Training Interaction: Working with Thoughts (thoughts game)
[103] Several units in the psychological program involve identifying and working with thoughts/self talk- whether biased, unhelpful, validating, or self-compassionate. While users receive psychoeducation and reflection, a gamified component can deepen this work by providing repetitive, engaging training that supports cognitive flexibility, emotional distancing (defusion), and internalization of more adaptive inner voice. Importantly, there is an evidence base for the efficacy of this sort of game suggested here (see the work of Guy Doron).
[104] The core concept is:
[105] we present the user with a “sorting task” of thoughts. Each time the task is different, based on the specific unit we are in. For example, in the “thoughts are not commands” unit, the user will need to identify biased thoughts versus non-biased thoughts. In the “self care” unit, the user will need to identify thoughts that promote self compassion  versus thoughts that do not promote it.
[106] We can also mix a few tasks together from several units.
[107] the user should get feedback if they identified correctly or not.
[108] Core therapeutic principles supported:
[109] Repeated exposure to maladaptive vs. adaptive statements
[110] Reinforcement of adaptive thinking and self-talk
[111] Defusion through repetition, labeling, and interaction
[112] Non-declarative learning that supports cognitive flexibility
[113] Key aspects to consider:
[114] This is not a full-blown game with progression/regression, but it can be a powerful way to practice the learned content in a fun and effective manner.
[115] The most important thing is making it fun and rewarding: Guy Doron's app can be an example for a dull and flat implementation. This deepends on availability of design and creative resources, but in theory there is an opportunity to integrate gamification elements like playful reinforcements, and humor. For example, biased thoughts could appear with exaggerated or caricatured visuals (e.g., the phrase “You’re not good enough” might be spoken by a cartoonish “inner critic” character like an old disapproving aunt). Having a golden statement (like "I'm good enough") that when the user correctly swipe, fills the screen, etc. Using positive imagery and positive reinforcement: positive imagery like brain, trees or clouds in the mechanic. In addition, "correct" responses trigger visual/audio feedback (growth, clarity, affirming messages), but avoid punitive framing for biased thoughts (not to create negative associations). These touches might be the way to make it enjoyable and more memorable.
[116] Swipe mechanic: e.g., accept/reject - was used effectively in research, can be replaced with a different mechanic of course.
[117] Speech integration: Optional - hear thoughts read aloud and/or speak them to deepen processing and externalization.
[118] Progress encouragement: Periodic feedback to reinforce positive change (e.g., “You’re training you inner voice to be more balanced”)
[119] Short, flexible play format: 2–3 minute sessions.
[120] Repeated explanations of the game's rational and desired outcome: direct or via embedded rationale cues like “When you practice this skill, you’re strengthening your resilience.”
[121] Note: In PD this game also engages cognitive aspects like proceeding speed, fine movement and visuospatial abilities, that might need special consideration.
[122] Personal note from Ariel: I genuinely believe this format holds potential as subtle yet meaningful therapeutic support tool. If designed thoughtfully, it can help bridge the gap between theory and practice, making cognitive work more accessible, engaging, and emotionally resonant for users.
[123] Pre or post-activity reflection
[124] Before or after activities (also outside the psychological program) we may define a reflection. This will be in the form of one of the following:
[125] Showing just text (and the user is invited to think about it)
[126] Note that ideally there would be some mechanism to make sure the user indeed takes a few seconds to think instead of just clicking next
[127] asking a question and letting the patient choose from a list of answers
[128] Note that there can be 1-3 questions per activity
[129] Note that the app should present a feedback based on the answer selected for each question (ideally, we can define a few response options and randomly show one so that the user is not always presented with the same feedback)
[130] Examples – after a mindfulness practice, ask if they feel more relaxed; after the anchor lesson ask a few questions such as “do you ever feel more anxious when your medication starts to wear off? (reply) “do you ever feel that stress makes symptoms more prominent?” (reply) “do you sometimes feel that it is hard to relax? (reply)” and then based on the answers, show a connecting sentence “in the following days we will share some practical tools you can use when …”.
[131] Logic – the app should allow to define if the post-activity reflection is shown:
[132] Always
[133] Only the first X times this activity is done
[134] Skip option – the app should allow users to skip the reflection and continue to the next activity
[135] Note that a similar structure can also be used for activities without prior educational content displayed (e.g. - in the first unit, one activity is that the user is reminded of the motivation they chose on day 1 and is invited to reflect how today’s daily activity can be relevant to achieve this motivation)
[136] Note that it makes sense for the app to remember the replies and use this information in the care coordinator.
[137] Keep
[138] To help facilitate knowledge assimilation, we will offer user the ability to “keep” 1-3 key points from education pieces they view or practices they perform. These will be pre-defined and the user will just be able to select them. the user will be able to access these “keeps” in the library, and each “keep” will link to the original education or practice it was taken from.
[139] Select Motivation
[140] In the first unit, the user will be asked to select a main motivation for using the app (out of a predetermined list)
[141] Consider option to let the user choose up to 3 main motivations
[142] This app needs to “remember” this selection so that future messaging to the user can relate back to this motivation selection (for example – write a message after completing a physical therapy sequence connecting it with the selected motivation).
[143] The motivation(s) selected do not need to appear consistently anywhere after the selection (meaning there is no need to display it somewhere or let the user edit it, etc.)
[144] A note on the care coordinator
[145] The care coordinator should collect the patients’ choices throughout the use of the app and use that to provide patient-specific framing of the activities (both within the psych program and outside of it). For example, if a patient indicates that spending time with family is a strong value/motivation, the care coordinator will use that to create motivational and suggestive introductions to activities within the app (example – working on your strength may feel like a lot sometimes, but remember this is not just a movement goal, you are doing it so that you can have more quality time with your family).

[152] Program Detailed Structure
[153] Throughout the program, some elements are considered core, while others are optional for those interested in exploring more.
[154] Unit 1: Welcome
[155] Anchor
[156] a brief explanation that PD affects not only movement (its not all physical symptoms), but also mood, attention, stress, confidence, motivation, energy fluctuation, frustration tolerance, and social comfort and that these are common and understandable consequences of the condition.
[157] These difficulties are not always in the foreground when people think about Parkinson’s or even in medical appointments (people tend to think mainly on the physical symptoms), yet living with them can be complex and burdensome. Addressing them and learning how to respond to them a little more effectively (a little bit every day… small steps make a big difference…) can make a meaningful difference in daily life.
[158] a normalization of common reactions: frustration, grief, worry, slower initiation, embarrassment, reduced spontaneity, self-blame
[159] The psychological program is designed to help you build practical skills for these different challenges.
[160] Sometimes this means learning how to respond differently to difficult thoughts or worries.
[161] Sometimes it means learning ways to calm the body or regulate stress.
[162] Sometimes it means training attention and mindfulness.
[163] Sometimes it means strengthening self-compassion, confidence, or social participation.
[164] And sometimes it means finding ways to stay engaged with the people, activities, and values that matter to you.
[165] Throughout the program, you will learn simple tools, practice them, and might gradually discover which ones work best for you.
[166] Emphasize that the app is built around repetition, flexibility, experimentation, and small steps rather than perfection.
[167] The overall message is that even a few minutes of practice can be a meaningful investment in yourself and your daily life.
[168] ***Perhaps we could also note that this is a gradual learning process (a few minutes a day). Each week adds another small piece to the puzzle. On its own, each step may seem simple, but over time these small pieces can add up to something meaningful.
[169] a simple explanation that the app is built around repetition, flexibility, and small steps. The tone should be: there is a target, but we protect continuity. The framing is positive and encouraging: even a few minutes in the app is already a meaningful step, a few minutes invested in self-care.
[170] Reinforce that every interaction with the app is already a meaningful act of managing Parkinson's. we want to frame each completed activity as active participation in one's health, helping users begin to see themselves as people who are actively taking care of themselves.
[171] Content can and should use stories/examples (e.g., someone who wants to walk more safely, someone who wants to feel more confident speaking, someone who wants to stay connected and independent).

[174] Unit 2: Action before motivation
[175] Anchor:
[176] Meaningful action often needs to come before motivation feels strong enough. This is especially important in PD, where low momentum, apathy, discouragement, and reduced spontaneity can contribute to a passive loop.
[177] It's really hard to increase motivation. Waiting for motivation to come is unhelpful and impractical – the common confusion is we often treat motivation as if it’s the fuel we need before we begin. But in many cases, action is what generates motivation, not the other way around. You might start feeling more motivated once you are already engaged in activities.  However, it is possible to get help to get started.
[178] difficulty initiating does not mean laziness or lack of character. The first step is often harder than the second and third (like a train, it is hardest to get moving at the beginning, but once it starts, it carries itself more easily).
[179] explain the cycle in which low action leads to lower confidence and lower mood, which then makes future action less likely. Explain why often the thing that is most within the patient’s control is the action. It should clarify that the app’s own content can count as meaningful action: movement can count as health and accomplishment, speech work can count as connection, cognitive work can count as engagement and competence, and occupational strategies can count as independence.
[180] Waiting for motivation or the “right” feeling before acting can become a trap. This can happen to anyone, but it is especially relevant in Parkinson’s, where motivation, energy, and mood can fluctuate due to the condition itself. A person may think, “I’ll start when I feel more ready, less tired, or more motivated,” but these internal states are not fully under voluntary control. As a result, action gets delayed. Over time, this leads to less activity, less sense of reward, and less momentum, which can lower mood and confidence and make starting feel even harder.

[210] Unit 6: Mindfulness and present-moment awareness
[211] Anchor
[212] The anchor lesson should provide a clear and practical foundation for mindfulness, keeping the explanation relevant and grounded without becoming overly theoretical. It may include brief scientific context (including in PD) and simple everyday examples to support understanding and application.
[213] It should explain that the mind often moves into prediction, self-evaluation, discouragement, worries about the future, or regrets about the past. Returning attention to the present moment can help reduce distress and allow for fuller engagement with daily life.
[214] Introduce mindfulness in daily life and its relevance in PD. Practices such as awareness of breath, body scan, mindful walking, and mindful eating can help users respond with awareness rather than react automatically to internal or external stressors. Emphasize that mindfulness is not only a formal practice, but an ongoing way of relating to experience – gently, kindly, and repeatedly bringing attention back to the present moment.
[215] Link this to the broader role of attention in PD. Many actions that were once automatic may require more conscious attention, and learning to direct attention intentionally can support functioning in daily activities, such as walking through narrow spaces or speaking more clearly. In this sense, training attention becomes a meaningful and practical skill.
[216] Throughout the week, highlight that the emphasis is not on doing long or perfect practices, but on cultivating an attitude of patiently returning attention and finding a sustainable rhythm. Mindfulness can be applied directly within everyday activities, and brief practices before tasks (e.g., before walking or eating) may help make these actions feel more manageable and steadier.
[217] Users will be invited to rate the different practices after completing them. This serves both as a brief reflection that helps them notice what felt helpful, relevant, calming, or enjoyable and as a way for the app to learn what works best for them. [252] Unit 10 - Responding to Difficulty
[253] Anchor
[254] We want to acknowledge and validate the challenges of living with Parkinson’s disease. These challenges may involve physical discomfort, pain, stiffness, fatigue, tremor, or other symptoms. However, it is important to acknowledge that these experiences are rarely only physical. They often involve many interacting parts, including the thoughts we have about symptoms, how much attention we give them, tension in the body, changes in activity and participation, and emotional reactions such as frustration, discouragement, or worry. These experiences are a normal part of living with an ongoing condition.
[255] While we cannot always control what shows up, we often have some influence over how we respond than we initially realize. The same difficulty can lead us in different directions depending on how we relate to it.
[256] Introduce the discomfort cycle in a simple and visual way:
[257] A difficult experience appears (pain, symptoms, fatigue, frustration, worry, etc.)
[258] Thoughts and interpretations about the experience
[259] Increased attention to symptoms and distress
[260] Difficult or unpleasant emotions like frustration, worry, or guilt.
[261] Tension and activation in the body
[262] Pulling back from activities and participation,
[263] life feels smaller.
[264] Explain the cycle in a simple and relatable way. not overly scientific, analytical – take it lightly. The emphasis is on showing how these experiences can become connected and influence one another, while highlighting that there are many opportunities for flexibility and influence along the way.
[265] Diagram note: This model originates from the TGCT pain unit and will need to be adapted for Parkinson’s. It is included here as a preliminary example. In the animation we’ll want to show arrows influences between non-adjacent parts of the cycle as well (for example doing fewer meaningful activities may lead to fewer positive emotions)
[266] Emphasize that these reactions are understandable and human. Normalize them as common responses when difficulties and uncertainty show up. Different people may experience different parts of the cycle, and not everyone will relate to every part of it. The goal is not to analyze it too closely, but simply to recognize patterns that may sometimes be relevant so we can support ourselves most effectively.
[267] The encouraging news is that each part of this cycle is also an opportunity for influence. Throughout the program, users have already learned a variety of tools that can help them work with thoughts, manage attention, respond more effectively to emotions, regulate the body, stay engaged in meaningful activities, and reconnect with what matters most.
[268] This unit is an opportunity to bring these pieces together. Rather than learning new skills, users will revisit different parts of the cycle and choose the approaches that feel most relevant and useful for them right now.
[269] The goal is to create more flexibility, more choice, and more opportunities to continue participating in life even when difficulties are present.

[395] Appendix 3 – Apathy resources
[396] Apathy in PD is increasingly understood not as a mood problem like depression, but as a disorder of motivation and effort-based decision-making. While depression affects how someone feels (sadness, hopelessness), apathy reflects a disruption in the brain’s “cost-benefit calculator". The brain tends to overestimate how much effort a task will require and underestimate how rewarding it will feel. This bias fuels an “Underdo-It” cycle: a person decides it’s too much work to attend a coffee meetup, stays home, receives no rewarding dopamine boost from social interaction, and as a result finds the next activity even harder to initiate.
[397] Modern cognitive-behavioral approaches for apathy (CBT-A) aim to recalibrate this system by reducing perceived effort (e.g., focusing first on putting on walking shoes rather than completing a full walk), increasing reward saliency significance, and using structured “if-then” plans to bypass impaired initiation. By creating small, achievable “micro-wins” such as committing to just five minutes of activity patients can trigger modest dopamine releases that help gradually restore motivation.

[401] The shift toward effort-based decision-making represents a major evolution in how we treat apathy. Historically, doctors treated apathy like depression, but we now know they are neurologically different: depression is a "feeling" problem (sadness), while apathy is a "valuation" problem (the brain decides the reward isn't worth the effort).In Parkinson's, the brain’s "cost-benefit calculator" becomes biased. Because dopamine levels are low, the brain overestimates how much physical or mental effort a task will take and underestimates how good the reward will feel.
[402] This creates a self-reinforcing loop:
[403] The Trap: "It’s too much work to go to that coffee meetup. I’ll just stay here."
[404] The Result: Because you don't go, you get zero dopamine "spikes" from social interaction.
[405] The Consequence: Your brain's "reward system" remains dormant, making the next task feel even harder. This is the "Underdo-It" Cycle—a downward spiral of inactivity that actually makes the biological apathy worse.
[406] Modern CBT (sometimes called CBT-A for Apathy) focuses on recalibrating this internal calculator. Therapists work on three specific areas:
[407] Cost-Reduction Strategies: If a task feels like it costs 10 units of energy, how can we lower it to 2?
[408] Example: Instead of "going for a 30-minute walk," the goal is "putting on walking shoes." Once the shoes are on, the "activation energy" required for the walk is significantly lower.
[409] Reward Saliency: Apathetic brains "miss" rewards. Therapists use Savoring Techniques—asking patients to focus intensely on a specific positive detail (the smell of the coffee, the sun on their face) to "force" the brain to acknowledge the reward.
[410] External Scaffolding: Since the brain struggles with internal initiation, CBT-A uses "If-Then" planning (Implementation Intentions).
[411] Script: "If the clock strikes 10:00 AM, then I will stand up and walk to the kitchen." This moves the decision from the struggling frontal lobe to a more automatic response.
[412] The goal is to create "micro-wins" that provide just enough dopamine to fuel the next small action.
[413] The 5-Minute Rule: Patients are told to commit to an activity for just 5 minutes. Often, the act of starting provides the neurochemical "kick" needed to continue.
[414] Low-Reward Sensitivity Training: Research shows apathetic patients ignore low rewards. CBT-A teaches patients to "stack" rewards.
[415] Example: Don't just do physical therapy; do physical therapy while listening to a favorite podcast. The podcast provides an immediate, high-probability reward that "subsidizes" the effort of the exercise.

[419] Fear of further physical (or mental) deterioration is very common in people suffering from Parkinson’s disease. Progression of the disease is inevitable. Patients are often frightened by seeing other patients who are further in the disease and have more severe symptoms. However, the rate of decline as well as the course of the disease vary a lot between patients. It may be necessary to remind them repeatedly about this. On the other hand, it can be this unpredictable aspect of the disease that makes patients with Parkinson’s disease anxious. As a therapist, it is important to check whether a patient worries excessively about the (uncertain) progression of the disease, and if it is necessary to formulate (specific and realistic) treatment goals about this.
[420] Especially in patients with Parkinson's disease there may be specific circumstances related to the disease that trigger anxiety, such as medication wearing off, motor fluctuations, dystonia, freezing, or balance problems
